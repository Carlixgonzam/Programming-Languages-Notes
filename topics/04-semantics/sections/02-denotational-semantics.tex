\section{Denotational semantics}
\label{sec:denotational}

Syntax describes the form of the strings of a language, and semantics gives
meaning to its elements, usually following the grammar. Operational
semantics describes the behavior of each element on a machine, through
actions guided by the derivation. Denotational semantics instead defines a
map from symbols and grammar rules to mathematical domains, and computes the
value of an entity from its derivation.

\begin{definition}
Given a domain $D$, the \textbf{denotational semantics} of a language is a
partial function $m : \Sigma^* \to D$ that gives each word of the language a
value in $D$. The function is \textbf{compositional}: the meaning of a
construct is computed from the meanings of its parts.
\end{definition}

For an imperative language the domain of a program is a function on
memories: $m(\mathit{Exp}) : \mathit{Environment} \times \mathit{Store} \to \mathit{Store}$. The
examples below are written as Scheme functions over abstract syntax trees
written as S-expressions; each function is the semantic function $m$ of its
language.

\subsection{Booleans}

The language of booleans has expressions such as
$(x \mathbin{\&\&} \mathit{true}) \mathbin{||} (\mathit{not}\ y \mathbin{\&\&} \mathit{false})$.
The lecture contrasts it with the Haskell type \texttt{data Bool = True | False},
whose operations are defined by cases:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\mathit{False} \mathbin{\&\&} x = \mathit{False} & \mathit{True} \mathbin{\&\&} x = x \\
\mathit{False} \mathbin{||} x = x & \mathit{True} \mathbin{||} x = \mathit{True} \\
\mathit{not}\ \mathit{False} = \mathit{True} & \mathit{not}\ \mathit{True} = \mathit{False}
\end{array}
\]
The expressions are syntax; \texttt{Bool} and its operations are the domain.
The semantic function maps each expression to an element of the domain,
given the values of the variables:

\subsection{Numbers and expressions}

For $E ::= E + E \mid (0..9)^+$ the meaning is a natural number,
$m : E \to \mathbb{N}$:
\[
m\llbracket 0 \rrbracket = 0,\ \dots,\ m\llbracket 9 \rrbracket = 9 \qquad
m\llbracket N\, D \rrbracket = m\llbracket N \rrbracket * 10 + m\llbracket D \rrbracket \qquad
m\llbracket E_1 + E_2 \rrbracket = m\llbracket E_1 \rrbracket +_{\mathbb{N}} m\llbracket E_2 \rrbracket
\]
The $+$ inside the brackets is a symbol of the language; $+_{\mathbb{N}}$ is the
addition of natural numbers. The rule for $N\,D$ reads a number digit by
digit: $m\llbracket 123 \rrbracket = (1 * 10 + 2) * 10 + 3$.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{19}{28}

\paragraph{Infix expressions.} For the grammar $E ::= E + T \mid T$,
$T ::= T * F \mid F$, $F ::= \mathit{num} \mid (E)$, where only $\mathit{num}$ has a value
($\mathit{num.val}$), there is one equation per rule (Table~\ref{tab:infix}).
Figure~\ref{fig:denot-tree} shows the meaning of each node of $2 * (3 + 4)$.

\subsection{Other domains}

The domain does not have to be numbers. The lecture gives several examples
where the meaning of an expression is something else.

\paragraph{Expression trees.} With variables $a$ to $z$, the meaning of an
expression can be its tree, built with $\mathit{leafInt}(n)$, $\mathit{leafVar}(c)$ and
$\mathit{tree}(\mathit{op}, t_1, t_2)$: $m\llbracket E + T \rrbracket = \mathit{tree}(`{+}', m\llbracket E \rrbracket, m\llbracket T \rrbracket)$,
$m\llbracket \mathit{NUM} \rrbracket = \mathit{leafInt}(\mathit{NUM.val})$ and
$m\llbracket \mathit{VAR} \rrbracket = \mathit{leafVar}(\mathit{VAR.val})$. This is the AST construction of
the previous block, written as a semantic function.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{41}{56}

\paragraph{Derivatives with pairs.} The meaning of an expression can be a pair
$\langle a, b \rangle$ where $a$ is its tree and $b$ the tree of its derivative with respect
to $x$:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\partial\llbracket E + T \rrbracket = \langle \mathit{fst}(E) + \mathit{fst}(T),\ \mathit{snd}(E) + \mathit{snd}(T) \rangle & \text{sum rule} \\
\partial\llbracket T * F \rrbracket = \langle \mathit{fst}(T) * \mathit{fst}(F),\ \mathit{snd}(T) * \mathit{fst}(F) + \mathit{fst}(T) * \mathit{snd}(F) \rangle & \text{product rule} \\
\partial\llbracket P^n \rrbracket = \langle \mathit{fst}(P)^n,\ n * \mathit{fst}(P)^{n-1} * \mathit{snd}(P) \rangle & \text{power rule}
\end{array}
\]
where $E$, $T$, $F$ and $P$ in the right side stand for their meanings. The slide
writes the product rule with $\mathit{snd}(E)$ and $\mathit{fst}(E)$ in the second
component; the operands are $T$ and $F$. It also writes $n * P^{n-1}$, where the
base is the tree $\mathit{fst}(P)$.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{60}{75}
The tests evaluate both trees of $x * x + 3 * x$ at $x = 2$ and get $f(2) = 10$
and $f'(2) = 7$.

\paragraph{Complex numbers.} A complex number has a rectangular form (real,
imaginary) and a polar form (angle, magnitude). The meaning is a triple
$(T, V_1, V_2)$ with $T = 1$ for rectangular and $T = 0$ for polar.
Addition adds the parts in rectangular form,
$E\llbracket E_1 + E_2 \rrbracket = (1, \mathit{real}(m\llbracket E_1 \rrbracket) + \mathit{real}(m\llbracket E_2 \rrbracket), \mathit{img}(m\llbracket E_1 \rrbracket) + \mathit{img}(m\llbracket E_2 \rrbracket))$,
and multiplication adds angles and multiplies magnitudes in polar form. The
functions $\mathit{real}$, $\mathit{img}$, $\mathit{ang}$ and $\mathit{mag}$ convert when the
argument is in the other form.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{82}{97}

\paragraph{S-expressions.} The S-expressions of Lisp, restricted to
$S ::= (+\ L) \mid (*\ L) \mid \mathit{num}$, $L ::= S \mid L\, S$, have two domains:
a number for $S$ and a list of numbers for $L$:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
m\llbracket S \to \mathit{num} \rrbracket = \mathit{num.val} &
m\llbracket S \to (+\ L) \rrbracket = \mathit{apply}(+, m\llbracket L \rrbracket) \\
m\llbracket S \to (*\ L) \rrbracket = \mathit{apply}(*, m\llbracket L \rrbracket) &
m\llbracket L \to S \rrbracket = \mathit{insertEnd}((), m\llbracket S \rrbracket) \\
m\llbracket L \to L\, S \rrbracket = \mathit{insertEnd}(m\llbracket L \rrbracket, m\llbracket S \rrbracket)
\end{array}
\]
The slide repeats $S \to (+\ L)$ in the rule for $*$.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{102}{108}

\paragraph{Sets of digits.} For $S ::= \{\} \mid \{\mathit{Nums}\}$,
$\mathit{Nums} ::= \mathit{num} \mid \mathit{Nums}, \mathit{num}$, the meaning of a set is a 10-tuple of
booleans whose $i$-th element says whether $i$ is in the set. The empty set is
a new tuple of $\mathit{false}$, and each number sets its position. Union,
intersection and complement work position by position:
$m\llbracket S_1 \cup S_2 \rrbracket_i = m\llbracket S_1 \rrbracket_i \mathbin{||} m\llbracket S_2 \rrbracket_i$,
$m\llbracket S_1 \cap S_2 \rrbracket_i = m\llbracket S_1 \rrbracket_i \mathbin{\&\&} m\llbracket S_2 \rrbracket_i$,
$m\llbracket S^c \rrbracket_i = \neg m\llbracket S \rrbracket_i$.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{112}{119}

\subsection{Programs}

\paragraph{Assignment sequences as memories.} For
\begin{lstlisting}
P  ::= begin As end
As ::= As ; A | A
A  ::= var := E
E  ::= E + T | ...
\end{lstlisting}
the meaning of a program is the memory after executing it. $[\,]$ is the empty
memory, $\mathit{Mem}[v/\mathit{var}]$ the memory after assigning $v$ to $\mathit{var}$, and
$\mathit{Mem}[\mathit{var}]$ the value of $\mathit{var}$ (zero if undefined):
\[
\begin{array}{l}
m\llbracket \mathtt{begin}\ As\ \mathtt{end} \rrbracket = \mathit{assign}\llbracket As \rrbracket\, [\,] \\
\mathit{assign}\llbracket As ; A \rrbracket\, \mathit{Mem} = m\llbracket A \rrbracket\, (\mathit{assign}\llbracket As \rrbracket\, \mathit{Mem}) \\
m\llbracket \mathit{var} := E \rrbracket\, \mathit{Mem} = \mathit{Mem}[m\llbracket E \rrbracket \mathit{Mem} / \mathit{var.val}]
\end{array}
\]
Each assignment is a function from memories to memories, and a sequence is
their composition.
\codelines{Scheme}{scheme/denotational.scm}{126}{140}

\paragraph{Translation as meaning.} With the same grammar, the meaning of a
program can be its translation to code for a stack machine, with the
instructions $\mathit{ASG}\ \mathit{var}$ (pop and assign), $\mathit{GET}\ \mathit{var}$ and
$\mathit{PUSH}\ \mathit{num}$ (push), and $\mathit{PLUS}$ and $\mathit{TIMES}$ (pop two values,
push the result). Code is built by appending instructions with
$\mathit{code.add}$:
\[
\begin{array}{l}
m\llbracket \mathtt{begin}\ As\ \mathtt{end} \rrbracket = \mathit{assign}\llbracket As \rrbracket\, \langle\rangle \\
\mathit{assign}\llbracket As ; A \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = m\llbracket A \rrbracket\, (\mathit{assign}\llbracket As \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE}) \\
m\llbracket \mathit{var} := E \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = (m\llbracket E \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE}).\mathit{add}(\mathit{ASG}, \mathit{var.val}) \\
m\llbracket E + T \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = (m\llbracket T \rrbracket\, (m\llbracket E \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE})).\mathit{add}(\mathit{PLUS}) \\
m\llbracket T * F \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = (m\llbracket F \rrbracket\, (m\llbracket T \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE})).\mathit{add}(\mathit{TIMES}) \\
m\llbracket \mathit{var} \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = \mathit{CODE}.\mathit{add}(\mathit{GET}, \mathit{var.val}) \qquad
m\llbracket \mathit{num} \rrbracket\, \mathit{CODE} = \mathit{CODE}.\mathit{add}(\mathit{PUSH}, \mathit{num.val})
\end{array}
\]
The slide writes the rule for $T * F$ with $m\llbracket E \rrbracket$ and
$m\llbracket T \rrbracket$, copied from the rule for $E + T$, and passes
$\mathit{CODE}_2$ to the function that produces $\mathit{CODE}_2$; the code of the left
operand has to be the input of the code of the right one. Figure~\ref{fig:stack-code}
shows the code for \texttt{y := x + 4} and the stack while it runs.

\subsection{Commands and fixed points}

This subsection goes beyond the slides. For SIMP the domain of a command is
a function from states to states, $\Sigma = \mathit{Var} \to \mathit{Val}$:
\[
\llbracket C \rrbracket : \Sigma \to \Sigma_\bot,
\]
where $\Sigma_\bot = \Sigma \cup \{\bot\}$ adds $\bot$ for programs that do not terminate.
Assignment, sequence and conditional are direct:
$\llbracket l := E \rrbracket\sigma = \sigma[l \mapsto \llbracket E \rrbracket\sigma]$,
$\llbracket C_1 ; C_2 \rrbracket = \llbracket C_2 \rrbracket \circ \llbracket C_1 \rrbracket$. The loop
$\mathtt{while}\ b\ \mathtt{do}\ C$ is the least fixed point of
\[
\Phi(f)(\sigma) =
\begin{cases}
f(\llbracket C \rrbracket\sigma) & \text{if } \llbracket b \rrbracket\sigma = \mathit{true} \\
\sigma & \text{if } \llbracket b \rrbracket\sigma = \mathit{false}
\end{cases}
\]
The approximations $\Phi^0 = \bot$, $\Phi^1 = \Phi(\bot)$, $\Phi^2 = \Phi(\Phi(\bot))$, \dots{}
describe the loop for at most $0, 1, 2, \dots$ iterations, and their limit is
its meaning. The two styles agree: for every command $C$ and states $\sigma$,
$\sigma'$, $\langle C, \sigma \rangle \Downarrow \sigma'$ if and only if $\llbracket C \rrbracket\sigma = \sigma'$.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Give the denotational semantics of $E ::= E + E \mid (0..9)^+$ and compute
$m\llbracket 12 + 30 \rrbracket$.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Solution.} The equations are the ones of this section. Then
$m\llbracket 12 \rrbracket = 1 * 10 + 2 = 12$, $m\llbracket 30 \rrbracket = 30$ and
$m\llbracket 12 + 30 \rrbracket = 12 +_{\mathbb{N}} 30 = 42$.

\begin{exercise}
Give the semantics of $S ::= (+\ L) \mid (*\ L) \mid \mathit{num}$ and compute
$m\llbracket (+\ 1\ (*\ 2\ 3)\ 4) \rrbracket$.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Solution.} $m\llbracket L \rrbracket$ for $1\ (*\ 2\ 3)\ 4$ is the list $(1, 6, 4)$,
because $m\llbracket (*\ 2\ 3) \rrbracket = \mathit{apply}(*, (2, 3)) = 6$. Then
$\mathit{apply}(+, (1, 6, 4)) = 11$.

\begin{exercise}
Give the meaning of $\{1, 3\} \cup \{3, 4\}$ and of $\{\}^c$ as 10-tuples.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Solution.} $\{1, 3\} \cup \{3, 4\}$ has $\mathit{true}$ at positions 1, 3 and 4,
and $\mathit{false}$ elsewhere. $\{\}^c$ is the tuple of ten $\mathit{true}$.

\begin{exercise}
Translate \texttt{begin x := 3; y := x + 4; x := x * y end} to stack code.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Solution.}
\begin{lstlisting}
PUSH 3, ASG x, GET x, PUSH 4, PLUS, ASG y, GET x, GET y, TIMES, ASG x
\end{lstlisting}
Running it leaves $x = 21$ and $y = 7$, the same memory as
the semantics of assignment sequences.

\begin{exercise}
Compute $\llbracket x := x + 1 ; y := x * 2 \rrbracket\sigma$ for $\sigma(x) = 3$.
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Solution.} The first assignment gives $\sigma_1 = \sigma[x \mapsto 4]$, and the
second $\sigma_1[y \mapsto 8]$.
